# Accuracy

Generated by `python ml/eval/report.py` over 40 rendered labels,
judged under rule pack `lmpc-2011@2026.07.01`.

> **What this is.** The corpus is rendered, so ground truth is arithmetic
> rather than a calliper reading and every millimetre below is exact. That
> makes this the right instrument for one question — given a clean image, is
> the geometry correct? — and the wrong one for the question that matters
> most: how the system behaves on real packaging, with foil, curvature,
> glare, and typography designed to be beautiful rather than legible. The
> golden set of photographed labels settles that. Until it exists, treat
> these as a floor, not a claim.

The last row is the one that matters. The reported interval is what the
engine refuses to convict outside of, so if the truth falls outside it the
interval is decoration and the whole `INDETERMINATE` gate is theatre.

## What a reference object is worth

| Frame | Labels | Height rule decided | Undecided | Mean error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ID-1 card in frame | 28 | 96.4% | 3.6% | 0.045 mm |
| No card, dimensions declared | 6 | 83.3% | 16.7% | 0.023 mm |
| Neither | 6 | 0.0% | 100.0% | — |

Two things follow, and the second is the one worth saying aloud.

A declared width is a usable ruler. Where an e-commerce listing carries
the package dimensions — which it must — the height rules can be decided
without anyone holding a card up to a screen, though less precisely than
with one, and the reported interval widens to say so.

With neither, the engine decides nothing at all. That is the design
working: no reference object means no ruler, and no ruler means no
accusation. The inspector is asked for a second photograph instead.
